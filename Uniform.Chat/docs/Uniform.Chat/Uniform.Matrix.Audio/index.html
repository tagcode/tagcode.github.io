    Uniform.Matrix.Audio

    Voice audio for calls, the first part of calling: microphone and speaker through NAudio, and the Opus codec through Concentus, a pure C# port of libopus. It also decodes and plays audio files, for audio messages. Nothing here knows about Matrix or the network; Uniform.Matrix.Calls builds calls on it.

    Voice frames

    Calls carry voice as 20 ms frames of 48 kHz mono 16-bit samples (VoiceFormat), each one an Opus packet:

    • VoiceEncoder encodes a frame, tuned for speech: the VoIP application, 64 kbit/s by default for full-band speech, and in-band forward error correction.
    • VoiceDecoder decodes a packet back into a frame; given no packet, it conceals the loss from the frames before.

    Concentus would switch to a native libopus where it finds one; both classes turn that off, so every platform runs the same code.

    Devices

    IAudioDevices opens a platform's microphone as an NAudio IWaveIn and its speaker as an IWavePlayer. AudioDevices.ForThisPlatform returns:

    Platform Class Through
    Linux AlsaAudioDevices NAudio.Alsa's AlsaIn and AlsaOut on ALSA's default devices, which desktops route through PipeWire or PulseAudio
    Windows WasapiAudioDevices NAudio.Wasapi, in shared mode
    Android AndroidAudioDevices in Uniform.Chat.Android Android's AudioRecord from the voice communication source, with the phone's echo cancellation and noise suppression, and AudioTrack as call audio on the earpiece, the loudspeaker, or a Bluetooth, wired or USB device; a Bluetooth speaker connected for music only gets it as media

    Devices record and play in their own formats. MicrophoneFrames turns any IWaveIn into voice frames, mixing channels to mono and resampling to 48 kHz; SpeakerFrames plays frames through any IWavePlayer, with a short buffer against uneven arrival and silence in the gaps. ALSA's default devices convert formats themselves; WASAPI's shared mode doesn't change sample rates, so on Windows the speaker resamples voice to the device's mix format, such as 44.1 kHz stereo, before handing it over.

    Hearing yourself

    VoiceLoopback plays the microphone back through the speaker, through Opus and a delay, to test the whole path without a call. The terminal client's /audiotest runs it:

    IAudioDevices devices = AudioDevices.ForThisPlatform() ?? throw new PlatformNotSupportedException();
using VoiceLoopback loopback = new(devices, TimeSpan.FromSeconds(1));
loopback.Start();

    Choosing devices

    AudioDevices.ForThisPlatform takes a microphone and a speaker by name: on Linux an ALSA device name such as pulse or plughw:CARD=MKII,DEV=0, on Windows part of the device's friendly name. ListMicrophones and ListSpeakers return the choices. The terminal client's Audio:Microphone and Audio:Speaker settings pass them on.

    An AudioChoice names the microphone and speaker a call uses, by the Name of each from ListMicrophones and ListSpeakers; null is the platform's default. The apps open their devices with it, so the call bar's menus can choose them and remember the choice. On phones, AudioChoice.Earpiece and AudioChoice.Loudspeaker are speakers too.

    On Linux, ALSA's default and pulse devices go through PipeWire's PulseAudio layer, which follows the PULSE_SOURCE and PULSE_SINK environment variables, so one process can be pointed at other PipeWire devices.

    Audio files

    AudioFile.Decode decodes a whole file into an AudioClip: 16-bit samples at the file's own rate, mono or stereo, with more channels mixed down to two. AudioFile.Detect recognizes the format by the file's first bytes, and AudioFile.IsPlayable tells from a MIME type or file name, before a download, whether a file is likely one to play.

    Format Decoder
    WAV, integer PCM of 8 to 32 bits or float NAudio's WaveFileReader
    MP3, MPEG-1, 2 and 2.5 layer III Mp3File, in C#, copied from the same author's Uniform.Audio.Sampling.Encoding; trims the encoder delay and padding a LAME header tells
    FLAC, up to 24 bits, and 32 without stereo decorrelation FlacFile, in C#, per RFC 9639
    Ogg Opus, as Element's voice messages are OggPackets reads the Ogg pages, and Concentus decodes at 48 kHz; the pre-skip and the last granule position trim the start and end, and the output gain is applied
    Ogg Vorbis NVorbis

    AAC, as in .m4a files, is not supported: such messages keep Open and Save. A clip is at most AudioFile.DefaultMaxSamples, 128 MB of samples, about 12 minutes of stereo at 44.1 kHz; a longer file throws NotSupportedException, and a broken one InvalidDataException.

    AudioClipPlayer plays a clip: play, pause, resume, seek and stop, with the Position played and Ended at the end. It converts the clip to 48 kHz stereo, OutputFormat, with NAudio's resampler, and opens the speaker anew for each start, on the thread pool. It plays on IAudioDevices.OpenMediaSpeaker, which is the call speaker on desktops; Android plays media on its media stream instead of the earpiece.

    AudioClip clip = await AudioFile.DecodeFileAsync("voice.ogg");
IAudioDevices devices = AudioDevices.ForThisPlatform() ?? throw new PlatformNotSupportedException();
await using AudioClipPlayer player = new(devices.OpenMediaSpeaker);
await player.PlayAsync(clip);

    Tests

    Uniform.Matrix.Audio.Tests runs mostly without devices: a tone through Opus keeps its pitch and level, a lost packet is concealed, a 44.1 kHz stereo microphone becomes 48 kHz mono frames, and queued frames reach the player unchanged.

    AudioFileTests decodes files in Resources/: MP3s of each MPEG version against mpg123's output, FLACs made by ffmpeg at different compression levels against their lossless sources, sample for sample, and Ogg Opus and Vorbis against libopus and libvorbis, through ffmpeg, by signal-to-noise ratio and exact length. AudioClipPlayerTests plays, pauses, seeks and ends a clip on a speaker that pulls in real time.

    PipeWireTests goes through real ALSA and PipeWire with virtual devices it creates for itself with pactl load-module module-null-sink: a 500 Hz and a 1000 Hz tone played into a virtual microphone must come out of a virtual speaker, through the loopback, at their pitch and level. The variables above point the test's process at those devices. It removes the devices afterwards, and skips itself where no sound server runs, as on build servers.

    To see what such a test hears, record a virtual speaker with a short latency and interrupt the recorder rather than kill it; otherwise its last seconds stay unwritten.

    parecord --raw --latency-msec=20 --device=test_speaker.monitor --channels=1 --rate=48000 --format=s16le out.raw
